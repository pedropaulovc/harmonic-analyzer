# Content strategy

The campaign needs an audience before it needs a launch date. Kickstarter's
[Creator Handbook](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/handbook/promotion) recommends
planning promotion from the beginning, lining up pre-launch support and building
a contact list. Its guide to
[telling the story](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/handbook/your_story) also
recommends showing how much has already been accomplished. This project already
has working CAD, manufacturing drawings and a structured machining curriculum.
Once shop work begins, it will add real log entries, chips and measured parts.

Content work should not turn the build into a second job. Capture each shop
session while it happens, then reuse that evidence across the blog, email,
short video and the eventual Kickstarter page.

## Channel roles

Each channel has one job:

| channel | job | destination |
|---|---|---|
| YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, TikTok | reach people who do not know the project | landing page |
| Blog / public build notes | preserve the useful account and prove sustained work | email signup |
| Email | retain the audience without depending on a platform feed | Kickstarter pre-launch page, once live |
| YouTube long-form | explain major technical milestones and establish credibility | landing page or pre-launch page |
| Machining and model-engineering communities | participate, ask and answer technical questions | relevant article or video, only when useful |
| Kickstarter updates | keep backers informed during the campaign and fulfilment | the next project milestone |

The email list is the common destination before Kickstarter approves the
pre-launch page. After approval, the landing page should offer both the email
list and Kickstarter's "Notify me on launch" action. Use tagged links so signups
and pledges can be traced to the source that produced them.

## One body of work, several outputs

The logbook is the source of truth for the machining process. A blog post may
explain a lesson from it, but must not become a competing record of dimensions,
results or progress.

After each shop session, preserve a small evidence packet:

- photographs of the setup, operation, result and measurement;
- a continuous clip of any operation that cannot be repeated honestly;
- short detail clips once the machine has stopped;
- the tool, material, measured result and any failed attempt in the logbook;
- a brief voice memo explaining what changed and what remains unresolved.

Capture daily when work happens. Do not promise a daily publication schedule.
Most sessions will contribute to a later story; they do not each need their own
post.

A useful public build note answers one question. "Why the bushing needed a
second setup" is a story. "More work on the bushing" is not. The same note can
supply the email text, photographs, a short-video script and part of a later
long-form video.

## Short video is the routine format

The default public video is a 30–60 second vertical edit that can run on YouTube
Shorts, Instagram Reels and TikTok. Keep one clean master without platform
watermarks, then add platform-specific captions and links at export.

A short should contain a complete micro-story:

| time | beat |
|---|---|
| 0–2 s | the visual or technical hook |
| 2–10 s | why this operation or part matters |
| 10–40 s | the setup, cut, failure or measurement |
| 40–55 s | the result and what it proves |
| final seconds | one call to action |

The hook should be concrete: a thin wall, an unexpected setup, a failed fit, a
measured error or a mechanism moving for the first time. Do not open with a logo
or a general project introduction.

Use one call to action. Before the Kickstarter pre-launch page exists, ask the
viewer to join the build notes. After it exists, ask for a launch notification.
Do not spend the end of every video asking for likes on several platforms.

### Longer YouTube videos

Long-form video is reserved for milestones that need explanation. A 5–10 minute
video is appropriate when the project reaches a result such as:

- the first complete machined part;
- the first working subassembly;
- an attempt at one of the three high-risk parts;
- a failure that changes the machining plan or book;
- the machine's first plotted curve.

There is no long-form publication quota. These videos reuse footage already
captured for the logbook and shorts. They exist to answer a question a short
cannot: is the work careful enough to support a shop manual?

The Kickstarter campaign video is separate. Follow the 2:00–2:30 structure in
[`page-outline.md`](page-outline.md); do not stretch a social clip into the main
campaign film.

## Camera workflow

Start with one phone rather than a two-camera production setup.

For an irreversible operation:

1. Record horizontally at 4K/30 fps with the rear 1× camera.
2. Frame wider than the final shot and keep the tool-workpiece interface near
   the centre so a vertical crop remains usable.
3. Mount the phone independently of the machine, outside the chip, coolant and
   machine-travel envelopes.
4. Lock exposure, focus and white balance before starting.
5. Start recording before the machine and stop after the spindle stops. Never
   reach for the camera during an operation.

Once the machine is safe, turn the same phone vertically and record the details
that make a short work: the surface, fit, measurement, failed part, corrected
part and a brief explanation. Do not repeat a real cut for the camera.

A wall-mounted arm is preferable to tripod legs in the work area. If the mount
cannot be permanent, use a weighted stand outside the operator's path. Use a
mechanical phone clamp, not a magnetic mount that can collect chips. One large,
flicker-free soft light placed across the machine will usually improve the image
more than a camera upgrade.

Record machine sound for texture, but narrate afterward in a quiet room. Live
commentary competes with hearing protection and the operator's attention. No
microphone or headphone cable may enter the working area.

A second phone or action camera becomes useful only after the workflow proves
itself. Its job is a close vertical angle on an operation that cannot be
repeated; the main phone remains the horizontal record.

## Editorial sequence

The early material should follow the actual skills ladder rather than tease the
finished machine indefinitely:

1. Why the analyzer is worth rebuilding, and what the existing CAD does and
   does not prove.
2. Learning the first operations and measuring the first real parts.
3. Turning early failures into changes in the machining plan and manuscript.
4. Completing subassemblies and comparing them with the CAD.
5. Tackling the cone gear, knife edge and small shaft journal named in
   [`positioning.md`](positioning.md).
6. Assembly, calibration and the first plotted output.
7. Preparing the book from operations that have actually been performed.

This sequence produces a visible accumulation of evidence. It also leaves room
for failure. A scrapped part is useful material when the account names the
cause, the measured result and the next attempt.

## Pilot before adding production weight

Begin with three short videos made from work that already needs to happen:

1. one visual introduction to the machine and the missing shop manual;
2. one complete operation with a measured result;
3. one technical problem, failed attempt or difficult setup.

Review the pilot before buying another camera or accepting a rigid publication
schedule. Track:

- email signups and Kickstarter followers attributable to each item;
- comments and questions from machinists, educators and likely readers;
- useful technical feedback;
- approaches from relevant creators or publications;
- the time spent editing compared with the work documented.

Views are diagnostic, not the goal. A clip that reaches many uninterested
viewers but produces no continued relationship has not built launch momentum.
If paid promotion is considered later, test material organically first and put
money only behind a message that has already attracted the intended audience.

## Creator and community outreach

Participate before asking anyone to promote the campaign. Share useful progress,
answer questions and point to an article or video only when it is directly
relevant to the discussion.

Approach creators around a result their audience would care about: manual gear
cutting, metrology, mechanical computers, workholding or science education. The
outreach package should contain:

- a concise project explanation;
- one strong short video and one substantial technical account;
- original photographs that they may reproduce with credit;
- the public logbook and repository;
- a sample chapter or drawing when one is ready;
- one specific request, such as technical feedback, an interview or coverage of
  a completed milestone.

Do not open with a request to advertise the Kickstarter. Give them enough real
work to decide whether the project is useful to their audience.

## Evidence, rights and safety

Every public claim about geometry or progress must be checked against the repo
sources listed in [`../AGENTS.md`](../AGENTS.md). The logbook records what was
actually machined; campaign copy cannot get ahead of it.

All photographs, diagrams, footage and narration must be original. The campaign
may cite the 2014 Hammack, Kranz and Carpenter book but may not reproduce its
photographs, diagrams or text. That restriction also covers social posts and
press material sent to creators. See [`risks.md`](risks.md).

Content never justifies an unsafe setup. Do not obstruct controls, enter a
machine's motion envelope, work around a tripod, repeat a cut for another angle
or divide attention during an operation. The camera documents the work; it does
not direct it.
